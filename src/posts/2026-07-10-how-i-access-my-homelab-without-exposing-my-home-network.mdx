I have a Dell OptiPlex 3000 Micro running as my homelab at home.

It hosts several personal services, but accessing a homelab from the internet is not always as simple as forwarding a few ports on the router.

Depending on the ISP, you may be behind CGNAT or other network policies that make inbound connections difficult or simply unavailable.

Because of that, I mostly rely on **Cloudflare Tunnels**.

If I ever need something closer to exposing a service or port directly, **[Pangolin](https://pangolin.net/)** is another option — but that is a topic for another post.

My current setup is mostly built around:

- [Cloudflare Tunnels](https://developers.cloudflare.com/tunnel/)
- Cloudflare Access
- [Dockflare](https://github.com/ChrispyBacon-dev/DockFlare)
- [Pocket ID](https://github.com/pocket-id/pocket-id)
- [Tailscale](https://tailscale.com/)
- [Oracle Cloud Free Tier](https://www.oracle.com/cloud/free/)

## Web access with Cloudflare Tunnels

For web applications, I use **Cloudflare Tunnels**.

The homelab establishes an outbound connection to Cloudflare, so I do not need inbound connectivity from my ISP, a public residential IP address, or port forwarding on my router.

This is especially useful because ISP policies vary a lot, and I do not want my remote-access setup to depend on whether a specific connection allows incoming traffic.

On top of that, I use **Dockflare** inside the homelab.

Dockflare makes publishing services through Cloudflare much easier because I can configure them directly using **Docker labels**.

Instead of opening the Cloudflare dashboard every time I deploy a new application, I can define its hostname and tunnel configuration directly in my Docker Compose stack.

Conceptually:

```text
Docker Compose
     ↓
 Docker labels
     ↓
   Dockflare
     ↓
Cloudflare Tunnel
```

This fits really well with how I like to manage infrastructure: configuration stays close to the service itself and can be version-controlled together with the rest of the stack.

## Authentication with Cloudflare Access

Some of these services are protected by **Cloudflare Access**.

Before reaching the application, I authenticate through OAuth2/OIDC.

My identity provider is **Pocket ID**.

The important detail is that Pocket ID does **not** run inside my homelab.

## Why some services live on a VPS

This separation came from experience.

When I moved, my homelab was literally turned off and packed inside my luggage.

At that point I realized how frustrating it was to need some of my personal services and discover that they only existed on a machine that was currently unavailable.

That changed how I think about what belongs in a homelab.

Today, I have a small VPS running on the **Oracle Cloud Free Tier**, where I keep the services that I consider part of my basic personal infrastructure:

- Pocket ID
- AdGuard Home
- Memos
- a few other small services that I want available even when my homelab is offline

The goal is not high availability.

It is simply making sure that the services I actually depend on are not tied to a computer sitting at home.

## Authentication should not depend on the homelab

Keeping Pocket ID outside the homelab also avoids a bad dependency loop.

I do not want to depend on my homelab being online in order to authenticate into the services that give me access to my homelab.

```text
Need homelab
    ↓
Need authentication
    ↓
Authentication runs on homelab
    ↓
Homelab is offline
```

By keeping Pocket ID on the VPS, my authentication layer remains available independently of what happens at home.

## DNS should survive too

The same idea applies to DNS.

I run **AdGuard Home** on the VPS rather than on the OptiPlex.

If I shut down the homelab, restart Docker, break a network configuration, travel, or simply unplug the machine, my DNS filtering continues working.

For me, DNS is infrastructure rather than just another homelab application.

## Memos lives there too

I also run **[Memos](https://usememos.com/)** on the VPS.

It has become my personal notes and quick-memo system, and it is exactly the kind of service I do not want to lose just because my homelab is unavailable.

That became especially obvious while moving.

If the machine is powered off, in a suitcase, or temporarily unreachable, I still want access to my notes.

So Memos belongs on the more stable side of the setup.

## SSH access with Tailscale

For SSH, I use a different approach.

I do not expose SSH publicly.

Instead, I use **Tailscale** between my devices, my VPS, and the homelab.

That lets me access the OptiPlex directly through my tailnet:

```text
Laptop
  ↓
Tailscale
  ↓
OptiPlex
  ↓
SSH
```

No public SSH port is required.

## The complete flow

For web applications running inside the homelab, the flow looks roughly like this:

```text
Browser
   ↓
Cloudflare Access
   ↓
Pocket ID
   ↓
Cloudflare Tunnel
   ↓
Dockflare
   ↓
Homelab service
```

The services that should remain available even when the homelab is offline live separately:

```text
Internet
   ↓
Oracle Cloud Free Tier VPS
   ├── Pocket ID
   ├── AdGuard Home
   └── Memos
```

And SSH takes another path:

```text
Laptop
   ↓
Tailscale
   ↓
Homelab
```

## The way I split responsibilities

In the end, the architecture became pretty simple.

**Homelab**

Compute and applications that I can tolerate being temporarily unavailable.

**VPS**

Identity, DNS, notes, and small services that I want available independently of my home infrastructure.

**Cloudflare**

The entry point for web applications without depending on inbound connectivity from my ISP.

**Dockflare**

Automation for publishing Docker services through Cloudflare Tunnels using Docker labels.

**Tailscale**

Private access to the infrastructure, especially SSH.

**Pangolin**

An alternative for cases where I need something closer to exposing a service directly.

But Pangolin deserves its own post.

## Final thoughts

This is definitely not high availability.

It is still a homelab, and things can break.

The main lesson for me came from moving and realizing that a homelab can become very unavailable very quickly when it is powered off and sitting inside your luggage.

Since then, I have tried to separate **things I enjoy self-hosting at home** from **things I actually depend on**.

The homelab can go down.

My basic infrastructure should not.
